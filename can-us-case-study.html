Making Canada–U.S. trade data easier to use by district

I designed an interactive dashboard that maps Canada's trade relationship across all 435 U.S. congressional districts, helping policy teams find clear, place-based evidence before meetings and advocacy calls.

Role Product Designer (UI, UX)
Tools Figma, WordPress, Illustrator, After Effects
Service Data Visualization
With 1 Data Scientist, 1 Economist
Background The Gap Research Approach Design Outcome Learned
01 — Background

The data existed.
It just was not easy to use.

In early 2025, Canada's policy teams needed faster answers about U.S. trade exposure. The question was not only which states mattered, but which individual congressional districts had real economic ties to Canada.

The Business Data Lab had built an allocation model that broke state-level trade numbers down by congressional district using industry employment, GDP proxies, and geographic weights. My job was to turn that model into something people could open, read, and use in the moment.

The model created a new level of detail. For the first time, the team could talk about Canada's trade footprint at the district level, not just the state level.

The brief

Make Canadian trade exposure readable at the district level, fast enough to use before a meeting.

03 — Research

Different users needed
different answers.

I spoke with stakeholders, policy team members, and economists. Everyone was working from the same data, but they needed it for different jobs.

Diplomat / Trade Representative

"I need one clear number before a meeting, and I need to trust it."

Needs: Fast lookup, a clear summary, and something easy to reference.
Policy Advisor

"I need to compare districts without rebuilding the same analysis every time."

Needs: Rankings, sector breakdowns, and quick comparisons.
Economist / Analyst

"Before I cite a number, I need to understand where it came from."

Needs: Method context, assumptions, and access to the source data.

"The hardest part was not the map. It was deciding what should be useful before anyone clicked."

The default view mattered. If the map opened blank, users had to work before they learned anything. If it opened with clear district patterns already visible, they could start understanding the story right away.

04 — Approach

The first version made users
choose too much upfront.

The early model was built around dropdowns: state, district, product type, breakdown, and value. It worked, but it asked users to know what they wanted before the tool had shown them anything.

There was another issue too. Jobs and exports lived in separate views, so users had to switch back and forth to compare them. That made a simple comparison feel harder than it needed to be.

Early model. The filters came first, and jobs lived in a separate tab.

I flipped the model. The map comes first, then state, then district. Jobs and exports sit side by side, and sector detail opens only when someone needs it.

The revised structure moves from national view to district detail, with deeper sector data on demand.

05 — Design & Decisions

Designed for quick reading
and clear comparison.

Most users scanned before they read. They needed one number to orient around, then a clear path into the details. I kept the hierarchy simple, the contrast strong, and the spacing open.

Goods and services are always side by side. A district with high export volume tells one story. A district where Canada represents a large share of exports tells another. Both needed to be visible at once.

Map-first default state

The dashboard opens with all 435 districts visible, so users can see patterns before searching.

Political overlay built in

A party toggle adds political context without leaving the map view.

Goods and services, always side by side

Both columns stay visible, turning a two-step comparison into one glance.

One number front and center

Each view starts with Canada's trade rank and total exposure, so the main takeaway is immediate.

Sector detail on demand

Industry detail stays tucked away until users need to search, sort, or go deeper.

06 — Outcome

Live internally.
Ready when the timing is right.

The tool is live internally and used by policy, BDL, and selected external stakeholders. Teams are using it to pull district data before meetings, briefings, and advocacy calls.

The public release is still on hold because Canada-U.S. trade is an active and sensitive file. The product is ready; the timing just needs to be right.

07 — What I Learned

What this project taught me.

Designing for people under time pressure means making the first view useful right away. Speed and clarity were not extra features; they were the product.

I also learned that understanding the data is part of the design work. Some ideas looked good on screen, but the model could not support them honestly, so they had to go.

And sometimes a product can be ready before the moment is. That is part of designing in a policy environment.
