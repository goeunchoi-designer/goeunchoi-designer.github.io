Making Canada's real-time GDP estimate readable by anyone

BDLNow is the Business Data Lab's weekly GDP nowcast for Canada. Powered by 45 indicators, updated every Monday, months ahead of Statistics Canada. I designed it end to end.

01 — Background

The model was ready.
Nobody outside the team could read it.

Official GDP data takes about 60 days to come out. By then, businesses have already made hiring and spending decisions on gut feeling. Our economists built a model that tracks 45 live signals and estimates GDP every week, updating every Monday.

My job was to take that model and turn it into something a journalist, policymaker, or CEO could read and trust in seconds.

The brief

Make real-time GDP estimates readable by anyone. Business executive or financial journalist. Without losing what makes the model credible.

02 — The Gap

The data existed.
The interface did not.

Real-time forecasting existed. It just lived inside private banks and academic papers. We had the model to make it public. Three problems made that harder than it sounds.

01

Built for Academics, Not Humans

The only public reference was the New York Fed’s nowcast. Brilliant economics. But reading it required a statistics background most people who need GDP data do not have.

02

Decisions Made on Guesswork

Businesses waited months for official data. Hiring and investment decisions happened on gut feeling in the meantime.

03

The "Invisible" Answers

Every time our economists shared early data, stakeholders asked the same thing: what does this mean? The model had the answers. The interface hid them.

The Design Mission

The model was the engine. The design had to be the bridge. A journalist, a CEO, or a policymaker needed to be able to use it without a statistics background.

03 — Research

Comparative Audit

I brought in colleagues from across the Chamber. Policy advisors, communications staff, membership managers. I sat them in front of the New York Fed's nowcast and asked them to find the GDP estimate.

Nobody found it quickly. Most were lost within thirty seconds. The page was built by researchers for researchers. Everyone else was an afterthought.

01 Flaw 01

The Mental Model Mismatch

My colleagues arrived looking for a conclusion. The page gave them a proof first and buried the answer in the middle.

02 Flaw 02

The Friction of Information Density

Everything had equal visual weight. Nothing told you where to look first. The cognitive load was enormous before you got near the number.

The NY Fed Staff Nowcast. The model BDLNow is built on. The interface BDLNow set out to rethink.

The UX Challenge

Clarity and credibility are not a trade-off. Show the conclusion first. Put the technical depth one click away.

04 — Design

Same model, completely different reader.

Every decision in BDLNow responds to a specific problem on the NY Fed page. The comparison below shows exactly what we changed and why.

NY Fed Staff Nowcast

Estimate buried in a sentence. "The nowcast for 2026:Q2 is 2.5%, with the 50% probability interval at [1.4, 3.7]%..." The number has no visual prominence.

Probability bell curve as the confidence tool. Meaningful to a statistician. Opaque to anyone making a hiring or investment decision.

Raw data series names throughout. "Manufacturing shipments of non-durable goods, seasonally adjusted." Not readable without an economics background.

No context for the number. Nothing tells you whether 2.5% is strong, weak, or surprising relative to what other forecasters think.

BDLNow

The estimate leads the page. Largest type on screen. One sentence below states the number, the weekly change, and the reference week in plain English.

Bloomberg consensus range as the confidence frame. Where professional forecasters sit, shown visually. Anyone can read it without a statistics background.

Indicators grouped by economic category. Consumption, Production, Labour, Financial. Named for what they mean in the economy, not what the data series is called.

Context is built into the layout. The Bloomberg range shows at a glance whether BDLNow's estimate is inside or outside professional consensus.

One rule drove every decision: conclusion first, context second. The answer is on screen instantly. The data behind it is one click away.

Inside the platform

Three sections. Each answering a different question.

Estimate vs consensus, always visible

BDLNow's line and the Bloomberg professional range in one chart. No toggling.

Hover for the exact weekly value

Tooltip shows the estimate and date for each data point.

Direction readable at a glance

Full quarter in view on load. No clicks needed to read the trend.

Grouped by category, not by data series

Consumption, Production, Financial, Labour. Named for what they mean, not what Stats Canada calls them.

Chart links directly to the data table

05 — Outcome

Live, public, and updated every week.

BDLNow launched in September 2025. It is free, open to anyone, and updated every Monday. The platform tracks two consecutive quarters and refreshes automatically when Statistics Canada publishes.

It is the first public Canadian nowcast of its kind. Model-driven, updated weekly, no editorial adjustment.

45

High-frequency indicators powering the weekly estimate. Retail, manufacturing, employment, commodities, and financial markets. All re-weighted every Monday.

06 — What I Learned

What this project
taught me.

Understanding the model was part of the design work. I sat with the economists until I could explain what drove the week-over-week change and why the Bloomberg comparison mattered to different readers. That knowledge changed almost every decision I made on screen. You cannot design the communication of something you do not understand.

Public tools carry a different kind of responsibility. BDLNow was built to be referenced in boardrooms and cited in news coverage. Getting the framing right, especially the distinction between a nowcast and a forecast, was a design problem. The copy, the hierarchy, and the visual structure all had to work together before anyone scrolled past the first paragraph.
