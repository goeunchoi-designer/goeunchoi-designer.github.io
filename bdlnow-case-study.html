Designing Canada's real-time GDP nowcast into something anyone could actually read

BDLNow is the Business Data Lab's real-time economic intelligence tool. Powered by 45 indicators, it delivers a model-driven GDP estimate for Canada updated every Monday, months ahead of Statistics Canada. I designed the platform end to end.

01 — Background

The model was ready.
Nobody outside the team could read it.

Official Canadian GDP data takes about 60 days to come out. By then, businesses have already had to guess on hiring and spending decisions. Our team at the Business Data Lab (BDL) wanted to fix this. Our economists built a smart model that tracks 45 live signals to estimate the GDP in real time, updating every single Monday.

My job was to design the platform around this complex data. I didn’t want to water down the economics. Instead, my goal was to take our team’s months of hard work and turn it into something a journalist, policymaker, or CEO could easily read and trust in seconds.

The brief

Design a public platform that makes real-time GDP estimates accessible to anyone, from a business executive to a financial journalist, without losing the rigor the model was built on.

02 — The Gap

The data existed.
The interface did not.

Real-time economic forecasting wasn't new. It lived behind closed doors at private banks and inside dense academic frameworks. We had the model to change that. But we ran into three problems that made it harder than it sounds.

01

Built for Academics, Not Humans

The only public reference was the New York Fed’s nowcast model. It was brilliant economics, but reading it required a statistics background. Most people who actually need GDP data do not have a PhD.

02

Decisions Made on Guesswork

Businesses making critical hiring or investment choices were forced to wait months for official government reports. They had to spend money based on pure gut feeling while waiting for data.

03

The "Invisible" Answers

When our economists shared early data, stakeholders always asked the same questions: What does this mean? Is it good or bad? How confident are you? The model had the answers, but the interface hid them completely.

The Design Mission

BDLNow was not just a data engineering project. The model was the engine, but the design had to be the bridge that allowed a journalist, a CEO, or a policymaker to actually use it.

03 — Research

Comparative Audit

I pulled in a group of colleagues from across the Chamber: policy advisors, communications staff, membership managers. No economists. I sat with them in front of the New York Fed's nowcast page and asked them to find the GDP estimate. What happened next told me everything I needed to know.

Nobody found it quickly. Most were lost within the first thirty seconds. Not because they weren't smart. The page was never designed for them. It was built by researchers for researchers, and everyone else was an afterthought.

01 Flaw 01

The Mental Model Mismatch

The page assumed you already knew what you were looking at. My colleagues arrived looking for a conclusion. The interface gave them a proof first and buried the answer somewhere in the middle.

02 Flaw 02

The Friction of Information Density

Everything on the page had equal visual weight. Raw data series, timestamped releases, probability curves. Nothing told you what to look at first. The cognitive load was enormous before you even got close to the number.

The NY Fed Staff Nowcast. The model BDLNow is built on. The interface BDLNow set out to rethink.

The UX Challenge

Clarity and credibility do not have to be a trade-off. The solution was progressive disclosure: the high-level conclusion obvious at a glance, and the deeply technical data exactly one click away for the people who needed it.

04 — Design

Same model, completely different reader.

The audit gave me a clear list of what was broken. Each decision in BDLNow responds to a specific problem on the NY Fed page. The comparison below shows the direct line between what we found and what we built.

NY Fed Staff Nowcast

Estimate buried in a sentence. "The nowcast for 2026:Q2 is 2.5%, with the 50% probability interval at [1.4, 3.7]%..." The number has no visual prominence.

Probability bell curve as the confidence tool. Meaningful to a statistician. Opaque to anyone making a hiring or investment decision.

Raw data series names throughout. "Manufacturing shipments of non-durable goods, seasonally adjusted" with timestamps to the minute. Not readable without a background in economics.

No context for the number. Nothing tells you whether 2.5% is strong, weak, or surprising relative to what other forecasters think.

BDLNow

GDP estimate is the dominant element. The number leads the page in the largest type. One sentence below it states the estimate, the weekly change, and the reference week in plain English.

Bloomberg consensus range as the confidence frame. Where professional forecasters sit, shown visually. Anyone can read it without a statistics background.

Indicators grouped by economic category. Consumption, Production, Labour, Financial. Named for what they mean in the economy, not what the data series is called.

Context is built into the layout. The Bloomberg range immediately shows whether BDLNow's estimate is inside or outside professional consensus. No extra interpretation needed.

The restructure followed one simple rule: deliver the conclusion first, then provide the context. Instead of forcing users to understand the math before they could see the result, BDLNow gives them the answer instantly. If they want to explore the data layers behind it, that information is ready and waiting exactly one click away.

05 — Outcome

Live, public, and updated every week.

BDLNow launched publicly in September 2025 through the Canadian Chamber of Commerce. It is available to anyone at businessdatalab.ca/bdlnow, updated every Monday. The platform tracks two consecutive quarters at a time and refreshes automatically once Statistics Canada publishes the official release for a given quarter.

The tool reaches policymakers, business leaders, journalists, and financial professionals. It is the first public Canadian nowcast of its kind: free, model-driven, and updated every week without editorial adjustment.

06 — What I Learned

What this project
taught me.

Designing for credibility and designing for clarity are not opposites. This project proved that. The answer was not to simplify the model. It was to structure the interface so readers who needed the headline got it first, and readers who needed the depth could find it without searching. Progressive disclosure is not about hiding complexity. It is about sequencing it correctly.

Understanding the model was part of the design work. I sat with the economists long enough to understand what a dynamic factor model actually does, what drives the week-over-week change, and why the Bloomberg comparison mattered to different types of readers. That knowledge changed almost every decision I made on screen. You cannot design the communication of something you do not understand.

I also learned that public tools carry a different kind of responsibility. BDLNow was not an internal dashboard. It was designed to be referenced in boardrooms, cited in news coverage, and opened by people making real economic decisions. Getting the framing right, especially the distinction between a nowcast and a forecast, was not a content problem. It was a design problem. The copy, the hierarchy, and the visual structure all had to work together to communicate that distinction before anyone scrolled past the first paragraph.
